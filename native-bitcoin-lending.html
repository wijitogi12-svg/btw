Lending

Native Bitcoin lending: borrowing without wrapping the collateral

The design goal is to let a borrower keep collateral on the Bitcoin base chain instead of converting it into a wrapped representation elsewhere.

All topics
Published8 August 2026
Updated8 August 2026
Author / EditorialBitway Brief Editorial Desk
SectionLending

What is being proposed

Bitway's lending product is described as allowing Bitcoin holders to borrow stablecoins while their collateral remains on Bitcoin's base layer. The enforcement mechanism cited is the discreet log contract, a construction that uses adaptor signatures and oracle attestations so that pre-agreed outcomes — repayment or liquidation — can be settled without publishing contract details on-chain.

A committee-based collateral manager is described as holding the encumbered coins under rules that restrict movement to the outcomes defined by the contract. Lenders supply stablecoins to pools and are compensated from borrower interest, with over-collateralisation as the primary protection.

Why it is technically interesting

Most "Bitcoin DeFi" today depends on wrapped representations, which concentrate custody and bridge risk in a single issuer or contract. Keeping collateral on the base chain removes one of those layers, and settlement transactions look like ordinary multisig spends, which limits information leakage.

The trade-off is that the price feed and the committee become the trust anchors. Oracle attestations decide whether a liquidation is valid; the collateral manager's members decide, in practice, whether the protocol can execute at all. Decentralisation of both sets is what determines how meaningful the "non-custodial" description is.

Risks and missing parameters

Liquidation risk is inherent: a borrower who posts volatile collateral against a stable liability can be liquidated in a sharp drawdown, which is when execution is least reliable. Oracle failure, committee collusion and stablecoin de-pegging are additional failure modes that users should price in.

Loan-to-value limits, interest rates and liquidation thresholds could not be verified from independent public sources at the time of writing. Without those parameters, no meaningful comparison against other lending venues is possible, and readers should be sceptical of any comparison that claims otherwise.
